## Main Findings

- Public reporting describes Paul Conyngham as using AI tools, sequencing, expert collaboration, RNA/mRNA expertise, and veterinary oncology oversight to pursue a bespoke cancer vaccine for Rosie.
- The case is not a reusable proof that AI cures cancer. Cautionary reporting emphasizes that human experts, sequencing infrastructure, immunotherapy context, and specialized oversight did the real clinical/scientific work.
- The human analogue should be a case-building and expert-routing workflow: pathology, staging, biomarkers, sequencing, trial search, molecular tumor board review, expanded-access discussion, and response/resistance tracking.
- FDA expanded access is a physician-led route for some patients with serious disease to access investigational drugs outside trials when criteria and oversight requirements are met.
- NCI biomarker testing resources explain that biomarkers may help doctors choose treatment, but interpretation belongs in clinical context.

## Claims Safe To Reuse

- The Rosie story can be framed as "AI-assisted expert mobilization," not "DIY cure."
- The useful human workflow is to assemble a complete case packet and qualified expert network.
- Tumor data can matter for advanced options, but testing must be clinically appropriate and interpreted by professionals.
- Expanded access and clinical trials are legitimate pathways to ask about when standard options are failing, but they are not guaranteed and require clinician involvement.

## Claims Requiring Caution

- Do not say ChatGPT cured Rosie.
- Do not imply a human patient can copy the veterinary experiment.
- Do not provide mRNA design, formulation, dosing, self-administration, procurement, or manufacturing instructions.
- Do not promise trial eligibility, expanded access, molecularly matched therapy, or benefit.
- Do not treat a public story as clinical evidence for human cancer efficacy.
