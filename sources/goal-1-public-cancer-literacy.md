# Goal 1 Source Cards: Public Cancer Literacy Infographic

Status: first-pass source set
Last updated: 2026-06-12

## Crawl Scope

Goal 1 needs a public-facing explanation of cancer that is accurate enough for experts and readable enough for everyday humans. This first pass prioritizes authoritative public-health sources, foundational cancer-biology frameworks, and high-level prevention evidence.

Primary questions:

- What is cancer?
- How do normal cells become cancerous?
- Why can cancer keep growing and spread?
- Which evidence-backed actions reduce risk at the population level?
- What must be framed carefully to avoid false certainty or blame?

## Synthesis

Cancer is not one disease. It is a family of related diseases in which cells acquire biological changes that let them grow, survive, invade nearby tissue, and sometimes spread through blood or lymph. At the root, cancer involves changes in how cells read and use biological instructions, including inherited variants, DNA damage, replication errors, epigenetic changes, tissue context, inflammation, immune evasion, metabolism, and microenvironmental pressures.

The public prevention message should be framed as risk reduction, not guaranteed prevention. Important risk-reduction levers include avoiding tobacco, limiting or avoiding alcohol, maintaining a healthy body weight, being physically active, eating a healthy dietary pattern, avoiding excessive UV exposure, reducing carcinogenic exposures, receiving cancer-preventive vaccines when appropriate, and using recommended screening. Some cancers happen despite excellent choices because age, inherited risk, random replication errors, infections, environment, inequity, and incomplete biological control all matter.

## Source Cards

### NCI: Understanding Cancer

- URL: https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/understanding
- Organization: National Cancer Institute
- Source type: official public education
- Domain: cancer definition, basic biology
- Reusable claims:
  - Cancer is a collection of related diseases that can occur almost anywhere in the body.
  - Cancer involves gene changes that can cause cells to grow/divide when they should not or avoid dying when they should.
  - Abnormal cells can form tumors, invade nearby tissue, and spread.
- Caution:
  - Keep wording broad because different cancers have different origins, drivers, and clinical behavior.
- Related pages:
  - `concepts/what-is-cancer.md`
  - `visuals/goal-1-infographic-brief.md`

### NCI: The Genetics of Cancer

- URL: https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/genetics
- Organization: National Cancer Institute
- Source type: official public education
- Domain: cancer genetics
- Reusable claims:
  - Cancer is a genetic disease in the sense that it is caused by changes in genes controlling growth and multiplication.
  - Cancer-causing changes may be inherited, caused by environmental exposures, or arise from errors as cells divide.
- Caution:
  - "Genetic disease" does not mean every cancer is inherited.

### NCI Dictionary: Cancer

- URL: https://www.cancer.gov/publications/dictionaries/cancer-terms/def/cancer
- Organization: National Cancer Institute
- Source type: official dictionary
- Domain: public definition
- Reusable claims:
  - Cancer describes diseases in which abnormal cells divide without control, can invade nearby tissues, and can spread through blood and lymph systems.
- Caution:
  - Dictionary definitions are concise; pair with mechanistic sources for the infographic.

### CDC: Preventing Cancer

- URL: https://www.cdc.gov/cancer/prevention/index.html
- Organization: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention
- Source type: official public health guidance
- Domain: prevention, screening, vaccines
- Reusable claims:
  - Many common cancer risks can be lowered through healthy choices.
  - Screening can find some cancers early when treatment works best.
  - Vaccines can help prevent several kinds of cancer.
- Caution:
  - Avoid implying all cancers are preventable.

- URL: https://www.cdc.gov/cancer/prevention/vaccination.html
- Organization: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention

- Domain: vaccination
- Reusable claims:

  - Hepatitis B vaccination can help prevent liver cancer.

### NCI: Alcohol and Cancer Risk

- URL: https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/alcohol/alcohol-fact-sheet
- Organization: National Cancer Institute
- Source type: official fact sheet
- Domain: alcohol, modifiable risk
- Reusable claims:
  - Epidemiologic studies show people who drink alcohol have higher risk of certain cancers than people who do not drink alcohol.
  - Risk rises with more alcohol for some cancers, and even light drinking can increase risk for some cancers.
- Caution:
  - Public messaging should avoid moralizing and should state population-level risk.

### NCI: Obesity and Cancer

- URL: https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/obesity/obesity-fact-sheet
- Organization: National Cancer Institute
- Source type: official fact sheet
- Domain: obesity, metabolic risk
- Reusable claims:
  - Compared with people at healthy weight, people with overweight or obesity have higher risk for at least 13 cancer types.
- Caution:
  - Avoid shame. Weight is affected by biology, medication, environment, food systems, socioeconomic context, sleep, stress, and access to care.

- Organization: American Cancer Society
- Source type: professional guideline and peer-reviewed guideline article

### IARC/WHO: European Code Against Cancer, 5th Edition

- URL: https://cancer-code-europe.iarc.who.int/
- Organization: International Agency for Research on Cancer / World Health Organization, European Commission
- Source type: evidence-based public recommendations
- Domain: public prevention framework
- Reusable claims:
  - The European Code Against Cancer lists 14 evidence-based recommendations for reducing cancer risk.

- Caution:
  - The code is designed for the EU population; use as a prevention framework while checking local guidance for other regions.

### Hanahan and Weinberg: Hallmarks of Cancer, Next Generation

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21376230/
- DOI: 10.1016/j.cell.2011.02.013
- PMID: 21376230
- Authors: Douglas Hanahan, Robert A. Weinberg
- Year: 2011
- Source type: foundational review
- Domain: cancer biology framework
- Reusable claims:
  - Cancer progression can be organized around acquired biological capabilities such as sustained proliferative signaling, evading growth suppressors, resisting cell death, enabling replicative immortality, inducing angiogenesis, activating invasion/metastasis, reprogramming metabolism, and avoiding immune destruction.
  - Genome instability and tumor-promoting inflammation are enabling characteristics.
- Caution:
  - Hallmarks are an organizing framework, not a complete causal map of every cancer.

### Hanahan: Hallmarks of Cancer, New Dimensions

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35022204/
- DOI: 10.1158/2159-8290.CD-21-1059
- PMID: 35022204
- Author: Douglas Hanahan
- Year: 2022
- Source type: major review
- Domain: updated cancer biology framework
- Reusable claims:
  - The hallmarks framework is a heuristic for distilling cancer complexity.
  - Newer dimensions include plasticity, epigenetic reprogramming, microbiomes, senescent cells, and broader tumor ecosystem context.
- Caution:
  - Use "emerging" language where evidence is still developing.

### Lancet / Global Burden of Disease: Cancer Attributable to Risk Factors

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35988567/
- DOI: 10.1016/S0140-6736(22)01438-6
- PMID: 35988567
- Year: 2022
- Source type: large observational burden analysis
- Domain: global modifiable risk burden
- Reusable claims:
  - In 2019, risk factors included in the analysis accounted for an estimated 4.45 million cancer deaths globally.
  - The leading included risk factors were behavioral, while metabolic risks increased substantially from 2010 to 2019.
- Caution:
  - Attributable burden estimates are model-based and depend on selected risk factors, exposure estimates, and assumptions.

## Claims Safe For First Infographic

- Cancer is many diseases, not one single disease.
- Cancer begins when cells acquire biological changes that let them break normal rules for growth, survival, and tissue boundaries.
- Cancer can grow because abnormal cells can keep dividing, avoid death, recruit blood supply, evade immune control, alter metabolism, and adapt.
- Cancer can spread when cells invade nearby tissue and travel through blood or lymph.
- Risk can often be lowered, not eliminated.
- Evidence-backed prevention levers include no tobacco, limiting or avoiding alcohol, healthy diet pattern, physical activity, healthy weight, sun/UV protection, reduced carcinogen exposure, HPV and hepatitis B vaccination when appropriate, and recommended screening.
- Some cancers occur despite good choices; age, inherited variants, random cell-division errors, infections, environment, and access to prevention all matter.

## Claims Requiring Caution

- "Root cause" must not be reduced to one universal cause.
- "Prevent cancer" should usually be phrased as "lower risk" unless discussing specific prevention such as HPV vaccination preventing many HPV-caused cancers.
- Lifestyle guidance should avoid blame because individual behavior is constrained by biology, environment, income, culture, access, and policy.
- Screening is cancer-type-specific and can have benefits and harms; do not tell individuals which screening to get without clinical context.
