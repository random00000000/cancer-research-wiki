# Non-Official Source Strategy

Status: operating research note
Last updated: 2026-06-12

CancerResearchWiki should not become a mirror of CDC, NIH, NCI, FDA, WHO, or any other official institution. Official sources are useful anchors, but they are not the full frontier of cancer biology, prevention, or therapeutic strategy.

## Why This Matters

Official public-health sources are often conservative, slow to update, shaped for broad audiences, and constrained by policy, legal, political, institutional, and communication tradeoffs. They can be excellent for baseline safety and consensus, but they are not enough for a wiki that aims to become a cancer research intelligence system.

The wiki should triangulate:

- Foundational biology papers.
- Peer-reviewed systematic reviews and meta-analyses.
- Major cohort and randomized studies.
- Specialist society guidelines.
- Independent research organizations.
- Global burden and epidemiology analyses.
- Mechanistic preclinical work, clearly labeled by translation stage.
- Failed hypotheses, negative trials, and controversies.
- Official summaries, used as one class of source rather than the final word.

## Practical Rule

For any important public-facing claim, try to pair:

- One readable public-health or clinical anchor.
- One peer-reviewed research source.
- One source that exposes uncertainty, effect size, limitations, or debate.

For any frontier or mechanistic claim, prefer primary literature and high-quality reviews over public agency summaries.

## Source Weighting

Do not blindly trust a source because it is official.

Do not blindly reject a source because it is official.

Score sources by:

- Method quality.
- Transparency.
- Replication.
- Sample size and population relevance.
- Conflicts of interest.
- Biological plausibility.
- Effect size.
- Date and whether it has been superseded.
- Whether the claim is stronger than the data.

## Red Flags

- A public page gives advice without showing evidence strength.
- A press release exaggerates a preclinical or early trial result.
- A single study is framed as a breakthrough.
- A guideline omits harms, tradeoffs, or access barriers.
- A mechanistic result in cells or mice is promoted as human treatment.
- A wellness claim uses cancer fear to sell a product.

## Good Default Mix For Goal 1

- Public readability: NCI, Cancer Research UK, ACS, WCRF/AICR.
- Prevention evidence: WCRF/AICR Continuous Update Project, ACS guidelines and attributable-risk studies, Lancet/GBD analyses.
- Infection prevention: Lancet Global Health infection-attributable burden papers, HPV vaccine population studies, H. pylori eradication meta-analyses.
- Mechanisms: Hallmarks papers, tumor microenvironment reviews, metastasis reviews, immunoediting reviews, genome instability reviews.
- Screening: USPSTF, ACS, NCI PDQ, plus papers on benefits, harms, overdiagnosis, and inequitable access.

## Answering Implication

When answering future cancer questions, agents should say where consensus exists and where institutional guidance may lag, simplify, or conflict with emerging literature. The tone should be rigorous, not conspiratorial: institutions are useful, but evidence is the authority.
