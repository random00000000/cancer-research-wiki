# Air Pollution, PM2.5, and Lung Cancer Evidence

Status: first-pass evidence synthesis
Last updated: 2026-06-12

This page supports Goal 1 by making outdoor and ambient air pollution visible as a cancer prevention pathway. Fine particulate matter, especially PM2.5, is not just a respiratory irritant. Long-term exposure is associated with lung cancer risk and can interact with other vulnerabilities. Prevention requires cleaner transport, energy, industry, wildfire/smoke response, housing filtration, worker protection, and environmental justice.

## Plain-Language Summary

PM2.5 means airborne particles small enough to reach deep into the lungs. These particles can come from traffic, combustion, industry, power generation, wildfires, cooking, heating, and regional pollution transport. Long-term exposure is associated with higher lung cancer incidence and mortality. The absolute risk for one person depends on dose, time, smoking history, occupational exposures, genetics, lung disease, and local particle composition.

Air pollution prevention cannot be reduced to individual willpower. Personal actions such as checking air quality, using filtration, reducing indoor smoke, and wearing well-fitting masks during severe episodes can help, but the highest-impact prevention levers are cleaner systems and protection for people with the least control over exposure.

## High-Value Public Message

- Cleaner air is cancer prevention.
- PM2.5 can reach deep lung tissue and contribute to inflammation, oxidative stress, DNA damage, and tumor-promoting environments.
- Air pollution can matter for never-smokers as well as smokers.
- Personal protection helps during high-pollution episodes, but policy and infrastructure drive population risk.
- Environmental justice matters: people with the least power often face the highest exposure.

## Source Cards

### PM2.5 Lung Cancer Incidence and Mortality Meta-Analysis

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28487493/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5522148/
- Source type: systematic review and meta-analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - PM2.5 exposure was associated with increased lung cancer mortality and incidence.
  - The meta-estimate was higher for mortality than incidence in this analysis.
- Caution:
  - Exposure modelling and adjustment quality vary across included studies.

### Outdoor Particulate Matter and Lung Cancer Meta-Analysis

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24911630/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4154221/
- Source type: meta-analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - Outdoor PM2.5 and PM10 exposure was prospectively associated with lung cancer mortality.
  - Particulate matter evidence supports outdoor air pollution as a cancer prevention target.
- Caution:
  - Air pollution is a mixture; focusing only on PM2.5 can hide source-specific risks and policy levers.

### Air Pollution and Lung Cancer Specialist Review

- URL: https://www.jto.org/article/S1556-0864%2823%2900601-9/fulltext
- Source type: specialist review
- Reusable claims:
  - Air pollution is a lung cancer risk factor and is relevant to never-smoker lung cancer research.
  - Prevention requires population-level exposure reduction.
- Caution:
  - Do not use emerging never-smoker mechanisms to imply that all never-smoker lung cancer is pollution-caused.

### PM2.5 Mechanism Review

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39578555/

## Mechanistic Notes

PM2.5 particles can reach small airways and alveoli. Depending on source and chemistry, particles can carry metals, organic compounds, and combustion byproducts. Proposed lung cancer pathways include:

- Oxidative stress and DNA damage.
- Chronic inflammatory signaling.
- Macrophage activation and cytokine release.
- Epigenetic and mitochondrial changes.
- Tumor promotion in cells with pre-existing oncogenic changes.
- Interaction with tobacco smoke, occupational exposures, and underlying lung disease.

The key public idea is cumulative pressure: air pollution can keep lung tissue under repeated injury and repair stress.
