# Risk Communication For Cancer Prevention

Status: first-pass goal-1 communication guide
Last updated: 2026-06-12

This page helps future agents explain cancer risk without distorting the science. It is especially important for the public infographic, where a single percentage can mislead if the denominator, baseline risk, or uncertainty is hidden.

## Plain-Language Summary

Cancer risk numbers can be true and misleading at the same time. A "50% reduction" can mean 2 fewer people out of 100, or 1 fewer person out of 10,000. A risk factor can be responsible for many cancers in a population even if most exposed individuals never develop that cancer. Good cancer communication should show the starting risk, the ending risk, the population, the time period, and the uncertainty.

## Core Rule

When an agent sees a cancer risk number, ask:

- Compared with what?
- In whom?
- Over what time period?
- What is the baseline risk?
- Is this relative risk, absolute risk, odds ratio, hazard ratio, or population-attributable fraction?
- Is the source measuring incidence, mortality, survival, detection, infection, precancer, or biomarkers?
- Is the claim observational, randomized, modeled, or mechanistic?

## Key Terms

| Term | Plain meaning | Agent caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Absolute risk | The chance of an event in a group over a time period. | Best public default when available. |
| Relative risk / risk ratio | The risk in one group divided by the risk in another group. | Can sound dramatic when baseline risk is low. |
| Relative risk reduction | How much risk fell proportionally. | Always pair with absolute risk reduction when possible. |
| Absolute risk reduction | Difference between two absolute risks. | Often the most decision-relevant number. |
| Number needed to treat/screen | How many people need an intervention for one additional desired outcome. | Depends heavily on baseline risk, follow-up time, adherence, and harms. |
| Population-attributable fraction | Estimated fraction of cases/deaths that would be avoided under a modeled exposure change. | Not a statement that any person's cancer was caused by one factor. |
| Odds ratio | Ratio of odds, often from case-control or regression studies. | Can overstate risk when outcomes are common; do not treat as absolute risk. |
| Hazard ratio | Relative event rate over time. | Not the same as "percent of people helped." |

## Natural Frequencies

Natural frequencies are often clearer than percentages.

Less clear:

- "This screening test reduces mortality by 25%."

Clearer:

- "In a group like this, about 4 in 1000 people may die from this cancer over 10 years without screening, compared with about 3 in 1000 with screening. The exact numbers depend on age, risk, and test schedule."

The numbers above are an illustrative communication pattern, not a universal screening estimate. Always tie numbers to a source and population before using them as evidence.

## Public Translation Patterns

Use:

- "Among people like those in the study..."
- "Over X years..."
- "Compared with..."
- "This changed risk from A to B..."
- "This is a population estimate, not a personal guarantee."
- "The absolute benefit is larger when baseline risk is higher."

Avoid:

- "Cuts risk in half" without the original risk.
- "Raises risk by 20%" without saying whether the baseline risk was high or low.
- "Responsible for X% of cancer" without explaining population-attributable modeling.
- "Prevents cancer" for observational associations.
- "Safe" or "unsafe" as a binary when risk depends on dose, duration, age, and context.

## Worked Examples For The Wiki

### Relative Risk Can Sound Bigger Than Absolute Risk

If a cancer death risk falls from 4 in 1000 to 3 in 1000:

- Relative risk reduction: 25%.
- Absolute risk reduction: 1 fewer death per 1000 people.
- Better public wording: "In this example, screening is associated with 1 fewer cancer death per 1000 people, a 25% relative reduction."

### A Common Small Risk Can Matter A Lot

If a common exposure slightly raises cancer risk, it can still create many cases across a country. This is why population prevention often targets tobacco, alcohol, air pollution, diet patterns, infections, and UV exposure.

Public wording:

- "Small shifts in common risks can prevent many cancers at population scale."

### A Rare Large Risk Can Matter Deeply For A Few

Some inherited syndromes or occupational exposures can create high risk in smaller groups. They may not dominate population-attributable burden but can be crucial for those affected.

Public wording:

- "Some people need specialized screening or prevention because their personal risk is much higher than average."

### Population-Attributable Fractions Are Not Personal Blame

If a model says 40% of cases are attributable to evaluated modifiable risk factors, it does not mean:

- 40% of patients caused their cancer.
- Every case in that bucket was individually preventable.
- The remaining 60% has no preventable component.

Better wording:

- "At population level, known exposures explain a large share of cancer burden. That is a prevention opportunity, not a blame statement."

## Infographic Implications

The first public infographic should not carry dense equations. It should include a small note:

- "Risk reduction is population-level, not a guarantee."
- "A percentage needs a baseline: 50% of what?"
- "Prevention works best when personal choices and public systems both reduce exposure."

A later visual can show:

- 1000-person icon array.
- Baseline risk versus changed risk.
- Relative risk label and absolute difference label.
- Population-attributable fraction as a separate community-level frame.

## Agent Checklist Before Answering A Risk Question

Before giving a number, include:

- The numerator and denominator if known.
- The comparison group.
- The time horizon.
- Whether the number is absolute or relative.
- Whether it applies to incidence, mortality, recurrence, infection, or biomarker change.
- The evidence stage and source type.
- A plain-language uncertainty note.
