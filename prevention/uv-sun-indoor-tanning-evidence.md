## Plain-Language Summary

Ultraviolet radiation can damage skin-cell DNA and contribute to melanoma and nonmelanoma skin cancers. Prevention is not about fearing daylight; it is about avoiding repeated UV injury, especially burns and indoor tanning. The strongest public message is layered protection: shade, clothing, hats, sunglasses, sunscreen, safer schedules, and no tanning beds. Skin changes that are new, changing, bleeding, painful, non-healing, or unusual deserve clinician evaluation.

## What Is Strong Enough For The Public Infographic

- Solar UV radiation is carcinogenic to humans.
- UV-emitting tanning devices are carcinogenic to humans and are not a safe way to tan.
- Indoor tanning is associated with increased melanoma and nonmelanoma skin cancer risk in systematic reviews and meta-analyses.
- Sunscreen has prevention evidence, but it should be presented as one layer, not permission for longer intense UV exposure.
- Shade, protective clothing, hats, sunglasses, sunscreen, outdoor-worker protection, and child/adolescent burn prevention belong together.
- Skin cancers can look different across skin tones, and visual checklists are not diagnosis.

## Mechanistic Model

UVB can directly damage DNA, including cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers and 6-4 photoproducts. UVA can generate reactive oxygen species and indirect DNA damage. DNA damage, imperfect repair, immune effects, inflammation, and selection of altered cells can contribute to cancer development.

Public simplification:

> A burn or tan means the skin has reacted to UV injury. Less repeated UV injury means fewer chances for skin-cell DNA damage to accumulate.

## Source Cards

### IARC: Solar And Ultraviolet Radiation

- URL: https://publications.iarc.who.int/Book-And-Report-Series/Iarc-Monographs-On-The-Identification-Of-Carcinogenic-Hazards-To-Humans/Solar-And-Ultraviolet-Radiation-1992
- Source type: international carcinogenic hazard review
- Reusable claims:
  - Solar radiation is carcinogenic to humans.
  - Solar radiation causes cutaneous malignant melanoma and nonmelanocytic skin cancer.
- Caution:
  - Hazard classification does not give a personalized risk estimate; risk varies by exposure pattern, skin type, geography, immune status, occupation, and history.

### IARC: Sunbeds And UV Radiation

- URL: https://www.iarc.who.int/news-events/sunbeds-and-uv-radiation/
- Year: 2009
- Source type: international carcinogenic hazard classification summary
- Reusable claims:
  - UV-emitting tanning devices were classified as carcinogenic to humans.
- Caution:
  - This supports "avoid indoor tanning"; it does not mean all outdoor sun exposure has the same risk or that sunlight should be feared absolutely.

### Indoor Tanning And Melanoma / Nonmelanoma Skin Cancer

- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8656707/
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34885049/
- Source type: systematic review and meta-analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - Indoor tanning was associated with increased risk for overall and early-onset melanoma and nonmelanoma skin cancer.
  - Dose-response and earlier exposure patterns increase concern.
- Caution:
  - Observational studies can be affected by recall and outdoor UV co-exposure, but results align with carcinogenic UV biology and wider reviews.

### Epidemiological Update On Indoor Tanning

- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9689757/
- Source type: review
- Reusable claims:
  - Reviews and meta-analyses consistently link indoor tanning with increased skin cancer risk.
  - Youth access and tanning culture are prevention targets.
- Caution:
  - Policy impact depends on enforcement, marketing, and local behavior patterns.

### Sunscreen And Melanoma Prevention: Nambour Trial Follow-Up

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21135266/
- DOI: 10.1200/JCO.2010.28.7078
- Year: 2011
- Source type: randomized trial follow-up
- Reusable claims:
  - Regular sunscreen use in the Nambour trial follow-up was associated with reduced melanoma outcomes.
- Caution:
  - This was one trial context; real-world benefit depends on correct use and not using sunscreen to extend intense exposure.

### Daily Sunscreen And Keratinocyte Cancers

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10475183/
- DOI: 10.1016/S0140-6736(98)12168-2
- Year: 1999
- Source type: randomized controlled trial
- Reusable claims:
  - Daily sunscreen use reduced squamous-cell carcinoma tumor counts in the Nambour trial.
- Caution:
  - Basal-cell carcinoma findings were less clear; sunscreen is one prevention layer.

### UV Radiation And The Skin

- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3709783/
- Source type: mechanistic review
- Reusable claims:
  - UVA and UVB can damage skin through different mechanisms, including oxidative stress and direct DNA photoproducts.
- Caution:
  - Mechanistic detail should not become individualized diagnosis.

### Mechanisms Of UV-Induced Mutations And Skin Cancer

- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8477449/
- Source type: mechanistic review
- Reusable claims:
  - UV irradiation causes multiple DNA damage types that can lead to mutations and skin cancer emergence.
- Caution:
  - Repair capacity, exposure pattern, skin type, and immune state modify risk.

## Public Wording

Use:

- "A tan is a sign of UV injury, not a shield."
- "No tanning bed is a cancer-prevention tool."
- "Sunscreen helps, but shade, clothing, hats, and sunglasses matter too."
- "Outdoor work and childhood sunburn prevention are systems issues."
- "A changing, bleeding, painful, non-healing, or unusual skin spot deserves medical evaluation."

Avoid:

- "Never go outside."
- "Sunscreen makes sun exposure safe no matter how long."
- "Only fair-skinned people get skin cancer."
- "Use this image/checklist to diagnose yourself."

## Infographic Implications

The flagship infographic can keep "Protect from UV; avoid indoor tanning." This companion visual should carry the deeper message:

- UV can damage DNA.
- Tanning beds are not safer.
- Protection should be layered.
- Children and outdoor workers need structural protection.
- Skin changes need clinical evaluation, not self-diagnosis.
