# Cancer Claim Filter

Status: first-pass public protection tool
Last updated: 2026-06-12

This page gives agents and readers a quick way to evaluate cancer prevention and treatment claims before they become advice. It is meant to protect the public infographic from hype while still leaving room for real frontier research.

## Public Rule

Do not turn a cancer claim into life advice until it survives five checks:

1. Human outcomes: Did it improve cancer incidence, mortality, recurrence, progression, symptoms, or quality of life in humans?
2. Absolute impact: How many people benefit or are harmed, not just what the relative risk says?
3. Replication: Was it seen by more than one group, population, or study design?
4. Harms and interactions: Could it cause toxicity, malnutrition, false reassurance, or drug interactions?
5. Care delay: Could believing the claim delay proven prevention, screening, diagnosis, or treatment?

If a claim fails these checks, classify it as preliminary, misleading, or dangerous instead of presenting it as guidance.

## Fast Verdict Table

| Claim type | Better classification | Public answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Kills cancer cells in a dish" | Preliminary | Interesting biology, not enough for health advice. Human dose, delivery, toxicity, selectivity, and outcomes still matter. |
| "Alkaline diet prevents or cures cancer" | Misleading | Healthy plant foods can be useful, but the pH theory is not a proven cancer-prevention or treatment mechanism. |
| "Detox cleanse prevents cancer" | Misleading | The claim usually lacks clinical evidence and can distract from evidence-backed prevention. Liver, kidneys, lungs, gut, and skin already perform detoxification biology. |
| "High-dose supplements prevent cancer" | Misleading as broad advice | Correct deficiencies when appropriate, but do not use high-dose supplements as general cancer prevention. Some trials found harm in specific groups. |
| "Cannabis treats cancer" | Dangerous if cancer-directed outside a trial | Cannabinoids may have symptom roles in some settings, but major oncology guidance recommends against cancer-directed use outside clinical trials. |
| "Ivermectin/fenbendazole cures cancer" | Dangerous if self-medicated or substituted for care | Preclinical signals and anecdotes are not enough. Human clinical evidence is not established, and self-medication can cause harm or care delay. |
| "Fasting/keto starves cancer" | Preliminary and context-dependent | Metabolic interventions are active research areas, but they are not general prevention or treatment guarantees and can be risky during cancer care. |

## Claim Cards

### Alkaline Diet Or Alkaline Water

- Verdict: misleading when framed as cancer prevention or cure.
- What is true: many alkaline-diet food lists emphasize fruits, vegetables, nuts, and legumes, which can fit a healthy dietary pattern.
- What is misleading: diet does not meaningfully alkalinize blood, and urine pH is not proof that tumor biology has changed.
- Human outcome status: no good evidence that alkaline diet or alkaline water prevents or cures cancer.
- Public-safe wording: "Eat more plant foods because the pattern is healthy, not because you can change body pH to defeat cancer."
- Source anchors:
  - AICR alkaline diet explainer: https://www.aicr.org/resources/blog/does-the-alkaline-diet-cure-cancer/
  - Systematic review of dietary acid load and cancer: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27297008/

### Detoxes And Cleanses

- Verdict: misleading as cancer prevention.
- What is true: the body has detoxification systems, including liver and kidney pathways, and some nutrients participate in normal metabolism.
- What is misleading: commercial "detox" programs often imply toxin elimination without specifying toxins, validated measurements, or cancer outcomes.
- Human outcome status: very little clinical evidence supports detox diets for toxin elimination or durable health benefits; cancer prevention evidence is absent.
- Public-safe wording: "Support normal biology with food, sleep, activity, hydration, and exposure reduction; do not buy detox promises."
- Source anchors:
  - Detox diets review: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25522674/
  - NCCIH detoxes and cleanses summary: https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/detoxes-and-cleanses-what-you-need-to-know

### Supplements For General Cancer Prevention

- Verdict: misleading as broad advice.
- What is true: supplements can be appropriate for diagnosed deficiencies, pregnancy-related needs, malabsorption, restrictive diets, or specific medical indications.
- What is misleading: "anti-cancer supplement" marketing often extrapolates from nutrients, antioxidants, cell studies, or observational associations.
- Human outcome status: randomized supplement trials have not established a general cancer-prevention benefit, and some high-dose supplements caused harm in some groups.
- Public-safe wording: "Treat deficiencies; do not treat supplements as a shortcut around evidence-backed prevention."
- Source anchors:
  - WCRF supplements recommendation: https://www.wcrf.org/research-policy/evidence-for-our-recommendations/supplements/
  - ATBC beta-carotene/vitamin E trial: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8127329/
  - Physicians' Health Study II vitamins C/E trial: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19116389/

### High-Dose Vitamin C

- Verdict: preliminary as cancer-directed therapy; not general prevention advice.
- What is true: intravenous vitamin C can reach pharmacologic concentrations that oral vitamin C cannot, and it is being studied as an adjunct in some cancer settings.
- What is misleading: "vitamin C cures cancer" collapses route, dose, tumor type, combination therapy, and trial phase into a false certainty.
- Human outcome status: older randomized oral vitamin C trials did not show survival or symptom benefit; IV and combination approaches remain under study.
- Public-safe wording: "Do not self-prescribe high-dose vitamin C for cancer; discuss trial evidence, route, risks, and interactions with an oncology team."
- Source anchors:
  - NCI PDQ high-dose vitamin C: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK121338/
  - NEJM oral vitamin C randomized trial: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3880867/

### Cannabis And Cannabinoids

- Verdict: symptom-support research is different from cancer-directed treatment.
- What is true: cannabinoids may help some people with symptoms such as nausea, appetite, pain, or sleep, depending on product, dose, jurisdiction, and clinical context.
- What is misleading: symptom relief or preclinical antitumor signals do not prove cancer treatment efficacy.
- Human outcome status: ASCO recommends against cannabis or cannabinoids as cancer-directed treatment outside clinical trials.
- Public-safe wording: "Cannabis questions belong in symptom-care conversations with clinicians; do not replace cancer treatment with it."
- Source anchors:
  - ASCO guideline: https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.23.02596
  - PubMed record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38478773/

### Ivermectin And Fenbendazole

- Verdict: dangerous when promoted as self-treatment or replacement for oncology care.
- What is true: some laboratory studies explore antiparasitic drugs as repurposing candidates, and clinical trials may test specific combinations.
- What is misleading: preclinical mechanisms, social-media anecdotes, and veterinary dosing do not establish human cancer benefit.
- Human outcome status: no conclusive large-scale human randomized evidence supports ivermectin as cancer treatment; fenbendazole human evidence is limited to reports and preclinical work.
- Public-safe wording: "Drug repurposing is a research question, not a reason to self-medicate or delay proven care."
- Source anchors:
  - Ivermectin oncology review: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11912-025-01704-z
  - Ivermectin trial record example: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05318469
  - Fenbendazole preclinical study reporting no support for further solid-tumor study in that model: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3580766/
  - Fenbendazole self-administration case series: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40605964/

### Fasting, Fasting-Mimicking Diets, And Ketogenic Diets

- Verdict: preliminary/context-dependent for treatment; not a general cancer-prevention guarantee.
- What is true: cancer metabolism is a serious research domain, and dietary interventions may affect insulin, glucose, ketones, inflammation, body composition, and treatment tolerance.
- What is misleading: "starve cancer" ignores normal tissue needs, cachexia risk, tumor heterogeneity, and the difference between animal/cell data and human outcomes.
- Human outcome status: human clinical evidence is still not strong enough for broad cancer treatment or prevention advice.
- Public-safe wording: "During cancer care, restrictive diets should be clinician-supervised; for prevention, emphasize sustainable evidence-backed patterns rather than extreme restriction."
- Source anchors:
  - Ketogenic diet systematic review: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33813635/
  - Plant-based and ketogenic diets review: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35797039/
  - Fasting narrative review: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9530862/

## Agent Response Pattern

When asked about a questionable claim, respond in this order:

1. Classify the claim.
2. Separate prevention, treatment, symptom support, and research.
3. Name the evidence stage: cell, animal, case report, observational, early trial, randomized trial, systematic review, guideline.
4. State the main uncertainty or harm.
5. Offer the stronger alternative: proven prevention lever, screening discussion, clinical-trial search, or oncology-team question.
