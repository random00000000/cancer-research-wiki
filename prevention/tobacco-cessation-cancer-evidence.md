## Plain-Language Summary

Tobacco smoke is one of the clearest, largest, and most preventable cancer causes, but public messaging must never turn tobacco-related cancer into personal blame. Nicotine dependence is biological, commercial, social, and environmental. Quitting helps at any age, earlier quitting helps more, and people often need repeated attempts plus support. Cancer prevention also means smoke-free spaces, lower secondhand smoke exposure, radon-safe homes, safer workplaces, and policies that reduce commercial tobacco exposure.

## What Is Strong Enough For The Public Infographic

- Tobacco smoke contains carcinogens that can damage DNA and drive lung cancer biology.
- Tobacco smoking causes cancer at multiple sites, not only lung cancer.
- Quitting lowers future cancer risk; earlier quitting usually yields larger benefit.
- Former smokers can remain at elevated lung cancer risk for years, so screening eligibility and symptoms still matter.
- Secondhand smoke and co-exposures such as radon, asbestos, silica, diesel exhaust, and alcohol can matter.
- Support beats shame: cessation is a treatment and systems issue, not a moral test.

## Mechanistic Model

Tobacco smoke exposes tissues to a complex mixture of carcinogens and toxicants. Some compounds are metabolically activated into DNA-reactive forms. DNA adducts, mutations, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, impaired immune surveillance, and repeated tissue injury can all contribute to cancer initiation and promotion.

Public simplification:

> Tobacco smoke can repeatedly injure cells and damage DNA. Quitting reduces new exposure and gives repair, immune, and tissue systems a better chance, but past exposure can leave risk behind.

## Source Cards

### IARC: Tobacco Smoke And Involuntary Smoking

- URL: https://publications.iarc.who.int/Book-And-Report-Series/Iarc-Monographs-On-The-Identification-Of-Carcinogenic-Hazards-To-Humans/Tobacco-Smoke-And-Involuntary-Smoking-2004
- Source type: independent international carcinogenic hazard review
- Reusable claims:
  - Tobacco smoke and involuntary smoking are linked to multiple cancers.
  - IARC describes synergy between smoking and several occupational lung carcinogens, between smoking and alcohol for upper aerodigestive cancers, and between smoking and HPV infection for cervical cancer.
- Caution:
  - Hazard classification does not give a single personal risk number; risk depends on dose, duration, timing, co-exposures, and baseline risk.

### Hecht: Tobacco Smoke Carcinogens And Lung Cancer

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10413421/
- DOI: 10.1093/jnci/91.14.1194
- Year: 1999
- Source type: peer-reviewed mechanistic review
- Reusable claims:
  - Tobacco smoke contains multiple carcinogens relevant to lung cancer induction.
  - Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and tobacco-specific nitrosamines are major mechanistic examples.
- Caution:
  - Mechanistic evidence explains plausibility and pathways; public risk communication should still use epidemiologic evidence for population magnitude.

### Jha: 21st-Century Hazards Of Smoking And Benefits Of Cessation In The United States

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23343063/
- DOI: 10.1056/NEJMsa1211128
- Year: 2013
- Source type: peer-reviewed cohort analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - Smoking is associated with large mortality hazards in contemporary cohorts.
  - Cessation before age 40 was associated with avoiding about 90% of the excess mortality risk from continued smoking.
- Caution:
  - This is all-cause mortality, not cancer-specific risk alone; the simple public lesson is that earlier quitting helps greatly, not that age 40 is a magic threshold.

### Pirie / Million Women Study: Benefits Of Stopping

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23107252/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3547248/
- DOI: 10.1016/S0140-6736(12)61720-6
- Year: 2013
- Source type: peer-reviewed prospective cohort study
- Reusable claims:
  - Continued smoking carried large mortality risks in a prospective study of about one million women in the UK.
  - Earlier stopping was associated with larger recovery of life expectancy than later stopping, while stopping later was still beneficial.
- Caution:
  - Cohort was women in the UK; transfer to all populations requires context.

### Park: Cancer Risk Following Smoking Cessation In Korea

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38319658/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10848071/
- DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.54958
- Year: 2024
- Source type: population-based retrospective cohort study
- Reusable claims:
  - Sustained smoking cessation was associated with reduced cancer risk after long-term follow-up, especially for lung cancer.
  - Quitting at any age helped reduce cancer risk, with earlier cessation before middle age showing larger lung cancer reduction.
- Caution:
  - The study population and health-system context were Korean; residual confounding and changes after quitting require careful interpretation.

### Reexamining Decline In Lung Cancer Risk After Smoking Cessation

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32603182/
- Year: 2020
- Source type: systematic review / modeling of cohort evidence
- Reusable claims:
  - Lung cancer risk declines after quitting, but former smokers can retain meaningful excess risk for many years.
  - The paper questions whether common screening windows exclude some former smokers who remain elevated risk.
- Caution:
  - Screening criteria are jurisdiction-specific and must not be personalized without clinical guidance.

### Smoking Cessation After Cancer Diagnosis

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38065242/
- Year: 2023
- Source type: systematic review
- Reusable claims:
  - Supporting patients with cancer to quit smoking should be part of cancer care.
  - Continued smoking after cancer diagnosis is associated with poorer outcomes in many cancer contexts.
- Caution:
  - Patients with cancer need clinical support; cessation messaging should not imply blame for prognosis.

## Public Wording

Use:

- "Quitting helps at any age; earlier is better, and support works."
- "Tobacco smoke can damage DNA and raise cancer risk across many organs."
- "Former smokers may still need screening conversations and symptom attention."
- "Tobacco prevention is policy, housing, work safety, marketing control, and support, not just willpower."

Avoid:

- "You caused your cancer."
- "If you quit, you are safe."
- "Only smokers get lung cancer."
- "Just use willpower."

## Infographic Implications

The flagship infographic should keep "No tobacco; support quitting" as a top-level prevention lever. A companion visual should carry the deeper message:

- Tobacco smoke creates repeated carcinogen exposure.
- Quitting lowers new exposure and helps at every age.
- Former risk can persist, so screening and symptoms still matter.
- Secondhand smoke, radon, work exposures, and alcohol can amplify risk.
- Public health should remove barriers and shame.
