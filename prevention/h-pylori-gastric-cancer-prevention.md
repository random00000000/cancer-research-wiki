## Plain-Language Summary

Helicobacter pylori is a treatable bacterial infection linked to many non-cardia stomach cancers. Long-term infection can inflame the stomach lining and, over years, contribute to a pathway from chronic gastritis to atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and cancer. Eradication lowers gastric cancer risk in infected people, especially when treatment happens before advanced precancerous changes and when baseline stomach cancer risk is high.

Screen-and-treat does not mean everyone should start antibiotics. It means a health system identifies the right population, uses reliable testing, chooses eradication therapy with local antibiotic resistance in mind, confirms cure when appropriate, and routes red-flag symptoms or high-risk findings to clinical evaluation.

## What Is Strong Enough For Public Education

- H. pylori is a treatable infection and a major upstream stomach cancer target.
- Eradication can reduce future gastric cancer risk in infected people.
- Absolute benefit depends heavily on baseline risk: geography, age, family history, gastric lesion stage, reinfection risk, and local cancer incidence change the expected payoff.
- Antibiotic stewardship matters because failed treatment and resistance can undermine prevention.
- People with alarm symptoms need medical evaluation, not a public infographic decision tree.

## Mechanistic Model

Persistent H. pylori can create chronic inflammation in gastric tissue. In some people, that chronic injury and repair pressure can move through:

1. Chronic active gastritis.
2. Atrophic gastritis.
3. Intestinal metaplasia.
4. Dysplasia.
5. Gastric cancer.

Progression is not automatic. Risk is shaped by bacterial virulence, host genetics, immune response, salt-preserved food patterns, smoking, age at infection, antibiotic resistance, reinfection, and access to diagnosis and follow-up.

## Practical Public Wording

Use:

- "H. pylori is a treatable infection linked to many stomach cancers."
- "In the right population, finding and treating H. pylori can prevent stomach cancer."
- "Testing and treatment decisions depend on local risk, symptoms, antibiotic resistance, and medical guidance."

Avoid:

- "Everyone should take antibiotics to prevent stomach cancer."
- "H. pylori treatment eliminates stomach cancer risk."
- "A negative symptom check means there is no stomach cancer risk."

## Safety Boundary

Persistent upper-abdominal pain, vomiting blood, black stools, unexplained anemia, swallowing trouble, ongoing vomiting, or unexplained weight loss should be evaluated by a clinician. Family history, prior gastric cancer, known intestinal metaplasia or dysplasia, and prior treatment failure also move this out of simple public education.

## Source Cards

### Cochrane Review: Eradication Therapy For Primary Prevention

- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7389270/
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32205420/
- Related earlier PubMed record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24846275/
- Source type: systematic review of randomized trials
- Reusable claims:
  - H. pylori eradication therapy reduced gastric cancer incidence among healthy asymptomatic infected individuals in pooled randomized evidence.
  - Evidence quality and geography need to be considered before generalizing.
- Caution:
  - Incidence prevention evidence is stronger than gastric cancer mortality evidence.

### 2025 Gastroenterology Meta-Analysis

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39824392/
- Journal page: https://www.gastrojournal.org/article/S0016-5085(25)00041-1/fulltext
- Source type: systematic review / meta-analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - Updated trial synthesis supports eradication therapy as gastric cancer prevention in H. pylori-positive individuals.
- Caution:
  - Use the paper for exact subgroup estimates; public text should avoid overprecision.

### 2025 BMC Gastroenterology Meta-Analysis

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40259215/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12010618/
- Source type: systematic review / meta-analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - Pooled evidence found lower gastric cancer risk after eradication, with a reported RR of 0.61 and NNT of 332.
  - Benefit was especially relevant in East Asian and other higher-risk contexts.
- Caution:
  - Absolute benefit changes with background risk, follow-up duration, and treatment success.

### Intestinal Metaplasia And Dysplasia Meta-Analysis

- URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2025.1530549/full
- Source type: systematic review / meta-analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - H. pylori eradication was associated with reduced gastric cancer risk in people with intestinal metaplasia or dysplasia in the included studies.
- Caution:
  - Precancerous lesions require clinician-directed surveillance decisions.

### IARC/NCBI Screen-And-Treat Cost-Benefit Chapter

- URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK615792/
- Source type: IARC/NCBI Bookshelf implementation review
- Reusable claims:
  - Decision models generally support screen-and-treat as cost-effective in many settings.
  - Optimal strategy varies by local gastric cancer risk, testing options, antibiotic resistance, resources, and population age.
- Caution:
  - Cost-effectiveness does not equal a universal individual recommendation.

### Gastric Cancer Prevention In China

- URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK615763/
- Source type: IARC/NCBI Bookshelf chapter
- Reusable claims:
  - High-risk regional studies help show why baseline incidence and age matter for prevention strategy.
  - Persistent H. pylori and precancerous progression are important targets in high-incidence settings.
- Caution:
  - Findings from high-incidence regions should not be flattened into one global rule.

## Research Questions To Continue

- Which age bands and risk groups produce the best balance of benefit, harm, and cost in screen-and-treat programs?
- How should agent answers explain urea breath tests, stool antigen tests, serology, biopsy testing, and test-of-cure without giving personal medical instructions?
- How should local antibiotic resistance data change first-line eradication options?
- How should screen-and-treat be paired with endoscopy in high-incidence regions?
- What reinfection rates matter most by geography and sanitation context?
