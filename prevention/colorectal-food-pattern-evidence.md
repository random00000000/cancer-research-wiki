## Plain-Language Summary

Colorectal cancer is a strong example of why food pattern belongs in root-cause prevention literacy, but the message must stay honest. Whole grains and fiber-rich foods are associated with lower colorectal cancer risk, while processed meat and high red-meat patterns are associated with higher risk. This does not mean one food causes or prevents an individual's cancer. It means repeated exposures can shift the colon environment over years.

The public message should be:

- Make fiber-rich foods easier to choose: whole grains, beans, vegetables, fruits, nuts, seeds, and other culturally familiar fiber foods.
- Limit processed meat as a routine pattern; treat red meat as a moderation signal rather than a shame signal.
- Keep screening separate from diet. Food pattern can reduce risk probability; screening can find early cancer or precancerous polyps.
- Food access, time, money, work, disability, school meals, kitchens, culture, stress, marketing, and local stores shape what people can actually eat.

## What Is Strong Enough For The Public Infographic

- Higher dietary fiber intake, especially cereal fiber and whole grains, is associated with lower colorectal cancer risk in prospective-study meta-analyses.
- Processed meat has a stronger cancer-warning classification than unprocessed red meat; red and processed meat intake shows a positive dose-response association with colorectal cancer in meta-analysis.
- A useful public mechanism is "colon exposure environment": gut transit, dilution of carcinogens, microbial short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, heme iron, N-nitroso compounds, salt/smoke/nitrite context, and high-temperature cooking products.
- Screening remains central because colorectal cancer can develop through a polyp-to-cancer pathway over years.
- Risk-reduction advice should never imply that people who develop colorectal cancer ate "wrong" or caused it.

## Effect-Size Anchors For Agents

| Evidence signal | Quantitative anchor | Public translation | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Whole grains | A dose-response meta-analysis reported a summary relative risk of 0.83 for an increment of three daily servings of whole grains. | More whole-grain foods are associated with lower colorectal cancer risk. | Observational diet data can be confounded by other behaviors and access. |
| Fiber pattern | The BMJ systematic review concluded that high dietary fiber intake, especially cereal fiber and whole grains, was associated with reduced colorectal cancer risk. | Fiber-rich food patterns are a colorectal prevention signal. | Do not turn this into a supplement promise or one-food rule. |
| Red and processed meat | A PLOS One dose-response meta-analysis reported RR 1.14 per 100 g/day increase in red and processed meat intake. | Higher routine intake is linked with higher colorectal cancer risk. | Intake measurement, cooking methods, and food-pattern clustering matter. |
| Processed meat classification | IARC classified processed meat as carcinogenic to humans and red meat as probably carcinogenic to humans. | Processed meat deserves a clear public warning; red meat deserves moderation language. | IARC classification is hazard identification, not an individual's absolute risk estimate. |

## Mechanistic Model

Protective-leaning pathways for fiber-rich whole-food patterns:

- More stool bulk and faster transit can reduce contact time with harmful compounds.
- Fiber fermentation can support short-chain fatty acids, including butyrate, which may affect colon-cell regulation and inflammation.
- Whole-food patterns can improve insulin sensitivity, weight stability where possible, and inflammatory tone.
- Whole grains and legumes bring multiple nutrients and food-matrix effects, not just isolated fiber grams.

Risk-leaning pathways for processed and high red-meat patterns:

- Heme iron can promote oxidative chemistry and lipid peroxidation in the gut.
- Processing and some cooking conditions can increase N-nitroso compounds, heterocyclic amines, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons.
- Salted, smoked, cured, or nitrite-treated products can create exposure contexts that are different from fresh unprocessed protein foods.
- Processed-meat-heavy diets can displace fiber-rich foods in the overall pattern.

Public simplification:

> Food pattern can change the colon's long-term exposure environment. Favor fiber-rich staples and make processed meat less routine, while keeping screening and symptom evaluation in the picture.

## Source Cards

### Dietary Fiber, Whole Grains, And Colorectal Cancer

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22074852/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3213242/
- Year: 2011
- Source type: systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies
- Reusable claims:
  - Higher dietary fiber intake, especially cereal fiber and whole grains, was associated with lower colorectal cancer risk.
  - Three daily servings of whole grains were associated with lower colorectal cancer risk in the dose-response analysis.
- Caution:
  - Dietary measurement is imperfect; food patterns cluster with socioeconomic status, health behaviors, and screening access.

### Red And Processed Meat And Colorectal Cancer Incidence

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21674008/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3108955/
- Year: 2011
- Source type: systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies
- Reusable claims:
  - Red and processed meat intake was positively associated with colorectal cancer risk.
  - Dose-response analysis reported increased risk per 100 g/day increase in red and processed meat intake.
- Caution:
  - Public wording should distinguish population risk patterns from individual causation.

### IARC Evaluation Of Red And Processed Meat

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26514947/
- Year: 2015
- Source type: expert carcinogenicity evaluation summary
- Reusable claims:
  - Processed meat was classified as carcinogenic to humans.
  - Red meat was classified as probably carcinogenic to humans.
- Caution:
  - Hazard classification does not mean processed meat has the same absolute risk as tobacco or asbestos; agents must explain hazard versus absolute risk when readers ask.

### IARC Monograph: Red Meat And Processed Meat

- URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK507972/
- Source type: monograph chapter / evidence evaluation
- Reusable claims:
  - Human evidence was reviewed across many studies for red meat, processed meat, and cancer risk.
  - The colorectal cancer evidence is the central public-health signal for processed meat.
- Caution:
  - Use the monograph for evidence context and mechanism hypotheses, but cite meta-analyses for effect-size claims.

### Dietary Intake Of Fiber, Whole Grains, And CRC In Cohorts

- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7274214/
- Source type: prospective cohort analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - Higher cereal fiber and whole-grain intake was associated with lower colorectal cancer risk in parts of the cohort analysis.
- Caution:
  - Treat as supportive, not stronger than the systematic review.

## Public Wording

Use:

- "Food pattern matters for the colon environment."
- "Fiber-rich foods and whole grains are a colorectal-cancer prevention signal."
- "Make processed meat less routine."
- "Screening still matters; diet is not a replacement."
- "Food choices are shaped by systems, not willpower alone."

Avoid:

- "This diet prevents colorectal cancer."
- "Meat causes your cancer."
- "Fiber supplements erase risk."
- "If you eat clean, you do not need screening."
- "Cancer is caused by bad choices."

## Infographic Implications

The public visual should show:

- A fiber-rich pattern as the positive direction.
- Processed meat limitation as the risk-reduction direction.
- Screening as a separate but complementary pathway.
- A systems box for access, culture, cost, work, school meals, stores, kitchens, and marketing.
- A symptom safety line: blood in stool, persistent bowel changes, unexplained anemia, or weight loss need medical evaluation.
