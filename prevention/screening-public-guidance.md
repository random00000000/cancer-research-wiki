# Screening: Public Guidance For Goal 1

Status: first-pass screening synthesis
Last updated: 2026-06-12

This page supports public-facing cancer literacy. It is not a personalized screening plan.

## Plain-Language Summary

Cancer screening means checking for cancer, or for abnormal cells that may become cancer, before symptoms appear. Screening can save lives for some cancers by finding disease earlier, and some screening can prevent cancer by finding and removing precancerous changes. Screening can also cause harms, including false positives, false negatives, anxiety, procedures, overdiagnosis, and overtreatment. Public answers should say "recommended screening" and point people to clinicians and current local guidelines.

## Public Rules Of Thumb

- Screening is cancer-specific. There is no single universal cancer screening test for everyone.
- The right screening depends on age, anatomy, personal history, family history, inherited risk, exposures, symptoms, and local guidelines.
- Symptoms are not a screening situation. New, persistent, or concerning symptoms deserve medical evaluation.
- Symptom action and early-diagnosis evidence is tracked in `prevention/cancer-symptom-action-evidence.md`.
- Screening decisions should weigh benefits and harms.
- Access matters: public-health systems should reduce barriers to evidence-backed screening.
- Detailed harm anchors are tracked in `prevention/screening-harms-and-tradeoffs.md`.
- Balanced non-CDC-heavy source cards and the public visual are tracked in `prevention/screening-benefit-harm-balance.md`.

## Effect-Size Anchors

Use these numbers for agent retrieval and infographic calibration, not for personalized screening schedules.

| Screening area | Effect-size anchor | Public translation | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lung LDCT | NLST reported 20.0% lower lung cancer mortality with low-dose CT versus chest radiography in a high-risk screening population. | Lung screening can save lives for people at high risk. | Not a general-population screening test; false positives and follow-up harms matter. |
| Colorectal endoscopic screening | A 2024 meta-analysis reported RR 0.80 for colorectal cancer incidence and RR 0.74 for mortality for sigmoidoscopy/colonoscopy screening versus standard care. | Some colorectal screening can prevent cancer, not only find it early. | Test choice, adherence, follow-up colonoscopy, and procedural risk matter. |
| Stool blood testing | Randomized trials of fecal occult blood testing showed colorectal cancer mortality reductions, with classic estimates around 15%-33% depending on annual/biennial strategy and adherence. | Stool tests can reduce colorectal cancer deaths when repeated and followed up. | A positive stool test usually needs colonoscopy; one-time testing is not the same as a programme. |
| Cervical screening | Reviews of organised cervical screening report large mortality reductions among screened women and invited populations, with effect depending on programme quality. | Cervical screening can find precancerous changes before cancer develops. | HPV vaccination reduces risk but does not automatically remove screening need. |
| Breast mammography | USPSTF evidence summary reported age-varying breast cancer mortality reductions, with clearer trial benefit in ages 50-69 than 40-49. | Mammography can reduce breast cancer deaths, but the benefit-harm balance changes by age and risk. | False positives, biopsies, overdiagnosis, anxiety, and dense breasts complicate messaging. |

## Current USPSTF Anchor Recommendations

These recommendations are US-focused anchors. Future agents should check for updates before giving date-sensitive screening guidance.

### Breast Cancer

- Source: https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/Page/Topic/recommendation-summary/breast-cancer-screening
- Current anchor: USPSTF recommends biennial screening mammography for women aged 40 to 74 years.
- Public wording:
  - "For many women in the US, breast cancer screening begins at age 40 and is usually every other year, but personal risk can change the plan."
- Caution:
  - Dense breasts, prior cancer, high-risk genetic variants, family history, chest radiation history, symptoms, and age over 74 require individualized discussion.

### Colorectal Cancer

- Source: https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/colorectal-cancer-screening
- Current anchor: USPSTF recommends screening adults aged 45 to 75 years for colorectal cancer; screening from 76 to 85 years is selective and individualized.
- Public wording:
  - "Colorectal cancer screening often starts at 45 in the US; several test options exist, and abnormal non-colonoscopy tests usually need colonoscopy follow-up."
- Caution:
  - Family history, inflammatory bowel disease, prior polyps, hereditary syndromes, and symptoms can change timing and test choice.

### Cervical Cancer

- Source: https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/cervical-cancer-screening
- Current anchor:
  - Ages 21 to 29: cervical cytology every 3 years.
  - Ages 30 to 65: cytology every 3 years, high-risk HPV testing every 5 years, or cotesting every 5 years.
- Public wording:
  - "Cervical screening can find precancerous changes before they become cancer. HPV vaccination reduces risk but does not replace screening for people who still need it."
- Caution:
  - Do not oversimplify by saying "all women" without considering cervix status, prior hysterectomy, immunocompromise, DES exposure, prior abnormal results, and local recommendations.

### Lung Cancer

- Source: https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/lung-cancer-screening
- Current anchor: USPSTF recommends annual low-dose CT screening for adults aged 50 to 80 years who have at least a 20 pack-year smoking history and currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years. Screening stops once a person has not smoked for 15 years or has a health problem that limits life expectancy or ability to have lung surgery.
- Public wording:
  - "Lung cancer screening is for people at high risk, especially based on smoking history and age. It is not a general screening test for everyone."
- Caution:
  - Requires shared decision-making and access to high-quality follow-up.

## NCI / CDC Screening Source Cards

### NCI Cancer Screening

- URL: https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/screening
- URL: https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/screening/patient-screening-overview-pdq
- Reusable claims:
  - Screening looks for cancer or abnormal cells before symptoms.
  - Several screening tests have been shown to detect cancer early and reduce the chance of dying from that cancer.
  - Screening has benefits and harms and should involve informed/shared decision-making.

### CDC Screening Tests

- URL: https://www.cdc.gov/cancer/prevention/screening.html
- Reusable claims:
  - CDC supports screening for breast, cervical, colorectal, and lung cancers as recommended by USPSTF.
  - Regular screening may find breast, cervical, colorectal, and lung cancers early, when treatment is likely to work best.

### Screening Harms And Tradeoffs Page

- URL: `prevention/screening-harms-and-tradeoffs.md`
- Source type: local synthesis
- Reusable claims:
  - Screening can save lives and can also cause false positives, false negatives, overdiagnosis, overtreatment, procedure harms, cost, and anxiety.
  - Screening answers should distinguish symptoms from screening.
- Caution:
  - Use current local guidelines for personal screening schedules.

### Screening Benefit-Harm Balance Page

- URL: `prevention/screening-benefit-harm-balance.md`
- Source type: focused synthesis / public visual support
- Reusable claims:
  - Screening should be explained as a cancer-specific benefit-harm balance, not as a universal good or universal harm.
  - Symptoms should be treated as medical-evaluation questions, not screening questions.
  - Absolute numbers are preferred when comparing benefits and harms.

## Non-Official And Trial Source Cards

### NLST Low-Dose CT Trial

- URL: https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1102873
- Source type: randomized trial
- Reusable claims:
  - Low-dose CT reduced lung cancer mortality in a high-risk population compared with chest radiography.
- Caution:
  - Requires high-quality nodule management, smoking cessation support, and follow-up capacity.

### LDCT Evidence Review

- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8589726/
- Source type: review
- Reusable claims:
  - NLST and NELSON support LDCT mortality reduction in high-risk groups.
- Caution:
  - Screening benefit is inseparable from eligibility criteria and follow-up.

### Colorectal Screening Meta-Analysis

- URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1364923/full
- Source type: systematic review and meta-analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - Endoscopic colorectal screening was associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence and mortality in pooled analyses.
- Caution:
  - Colonoscopy trial evidence and observational evidence should be separated when answering detailed questions.

### Fecal Occult Blood Trial

- URL: https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM199305133281901
- Source type: randomized trial
- Reusable claims:
  - Annual fecal occult-blood testing reduced colorectal cancer mortality in long-term trial follow-up.
- Caution:
  - Modern FIT tests differ from older guaiac FOBT; programme adherence and colonoscopy follow-up matter.

### Organised Cervical Screening Review

- URL: https://www.ejcancer.com/article/S0959-8049%2819%2930868-8/fulltext
- Source type: systematic review
- Reusable claims:
  - Organised cervical screening is associated with large reductions in cervical cancer mortality.
- Caution:
  - Effects depend on coverage, test quality, treatment of precancer, and equitable follow-up.

## Infographic Implications

The current infographic says "Some tests find cancer early or precancerous changes." That wording is safe and should remain broad.

Future infographic versions may add:

- A tiny "ask about screening" calendar icon.
- A note: "Screening depends on age and risk."
- A "benefits and harms" balance scale.

Avoid adding:

- Detailed age cutoffs inside a small public infographic unless there is enough space for caveats.
- A claim that screening prevents all cancer deaths.
- Any personal instruction such as "you should get X test now."
