## Plain-Language Summary

Ultra-processed foods are industrial formulations such as many packaged snacks, sugary drinks, reconstituted meat products, instant meals, sweetened breakfast products, and some ready-to-eat dishes. The public message should not be "never eat packaged food" or "every additive causes cancer." The safer message is: treat a high ultra-processed food pattern as a warning signal, especially when it displaces fiber-rich foods, whole grains, beans, vegetables, fruits, and minimally processed staples.

Cancer evidence is suggestive, not final. Prospective cohorts and meta-analyses link higher ultra-processed food intake with higher overall cancer and some site-specific risks, especially colorectal and breast cancer in several analyses. But the field still has major questions about causality, food classification, confounding, and which subgroups of ultra-processed foods matter most.

## What Is Strong Enough For The Public Infographic

- A high ultra-processed food pattern is a reasonable public risk-reduction target, especially when it means sugary drinks, processed meats, low-fiber packaged snacks, and ready-to-eat products dominate the diet.
- The cancer signal should be described as "linked with higher risk" or "associated with higher risk," not as proven individual causation.
- The strongest practical replacement message is positive: make minimally processed, fiber-rich foods easier, cheaper, and more normal.
- Food processing is not one thing. Some packaged or processed foods can still help people eat fiber, protein, or safe affordable meals.
- Food systems matter: price, shelf life, marketing, work schedules, school meals, disability, cooking equipment, and neighborhood stores shape intake.

## Effect-Size Anchors For Agents

| Evidence signal | Quantitative anchor | Public translation | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Overall cancer in NutriNet-Sante | A 10% increase in ultra-processed food proportion was associated with more than 10% higher overall cancer risk. | Higher UPF share was linked with higher cancer incidence in one large French cohort. | Observational; no proof that every UPF category is causal. |
| Colorectal cancer cohorts | Men in the highest fifth of UPF intake had 29% higher colorectal cancer risk than men in the lowest fifth in three US cohorts. | High-UPF patterns may matter for colorectal cancer, especially some ready-to-eat meat and sugary drink categories. | The overall association was not seen in women in that study; do not universalize one subgroup result. |
| Meta-analysis | A 2023 meta-analysis reported every 10% increase in UPF share was associated with 4% higher colorectal cancer risk. | Pooled evidence suggests a small colorectal risk signal. | Included study designs and definitions vary; causal pathways remain uncertain. |
| Randomized feeding mechanism | In a controlled inpatient trial, ultra-processed diets led to about 508 more kcal/day and weight gain compared with matched unprocessed diets. | UPF can drive overeating and weight gain, an indirect pathway relevant to cancer risk. | Short trial, small sample, not a cancer endpoint. |

## Mechanistic Hypotheses

Possible pathways:

- Low fiber and high refined carbohydrate patterns can change gut transit, microbial metabolism, insulin signaling, and inflammation.
- Processed-meat-heavy UPF categories can overlap with known colorectal cancer signals.
- Sugary drinks and energy-dense products can contribute to weight gain and metabolic dysfunction where environments make high intake likely.
- Food texture, energy density, rapid eating, hyper-palatability, and weak satiety signals may increase calorie intake.
- Additives, emulsifiers, contaminants, packaging chemicals, and processing byproducts are plausible but need stronger human evidence by compound and dose.
- UPF can displace protective food patterns: whole grains, beans, vegetables, fruits, nuts, seeds, and minimally processed staples.

Public simplification:

> Ultra-processed food is a pattern warning, not a purity test. Reduce the routine products that crowd out fiber-rich staples, and change the food environment so this is realistic.

## Source Cards

### NutriNet-Sante: Ultra-Processed Foods And Cancer Risk

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29444771/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5811844/
- Year: 2018
- Source type: prospective cohort study
- Reusable claims:
  - Higher ultra-processed food proportion was associated with higher overall cancer and breast cancer risk.
  - The authors called for further studies and did not prove causality.
- Caution:
  - Use as an important signal, not a settled causal estimate for all populations or all UPF categories.

### BMJ: Ultra-Processed Food And Colorectal Cancer

- URL: https://www.bmj.com/content/378/bmj-2021-068921
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38752573/
- Year: 2022
- Source type: prospective cohort study across three US cohorts
- Reusable claims:
  - Men in the highest fifth of ultra-processed food intake had higher colorectal cancer risk than men in the lowest fifth.
  - The association was strongest for distal colon cancer and for some UPF subgroups.
  - No overall association was observed among women in the same analysis.
- Caution:
  - Public messaging must include the sex/subgroup nuance and avoid claiming all UPFs have the same effect.

### Clinical Nutrition: Systematic Review And Meta-Analysis

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37087831/
- Journal page: https://www.clinicalnutritionjournal.com/article/S0261-5614(23)00099-7/abstract
- Year: 2023
- Source type: systematic review and meta-analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - Available evidence suggested a consistent association between UPF intake and overall and several cancer risks.
  - A 10% increment in diet proportion from UPF was associated with higher overall cancer risk in the pooled analysis.
- Caution:
  - Evidence was described as suggestive; definitions and study designs vary.

### Frontiers In Nutrition: Cancer-Risk Meta-Analysis

- URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2023.1175994/full
- Year: 2023
- Source type: systematic review and meta-analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - Higher UPF consumption was associated with increased odds of several site-specific cancers, especially digestive tract and some hormone-related cancers.
  - The analysis reported a small colorectal risk increase per 10% UPF share.
- Caution:
  - The authors called for more prospective and experimental studies to clarify causal pathways.

### Hall: Ultra-Processed Diets, Energy Intake, And Weight Gain

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7946062/
- Year: 2019
- Source type: inpatient randomized controlled feeding trial
- Reusable claims:
  - Participants ate more calories and gained weight during the ultra-processed diet phase compared with the unprocessed diet phase.
  - This supports an indirect mechanistic pathway through energy intake and weight gain.
- Caution:
  - This was not a cancer trial and should not be cited as direct cancer evidence.

### Food Processing And Cancer Risk In Europe

- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10009757/
- Source type: prospective European cohort analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - Food processing level may relate to cancer risk through obesogenic properties, nutritional quality, additives, contaminants, and packaging exposures.
- Caution:
  - Processing categories can mix foods with very different nutritional roles and social uses.

## Public Wording

Use:

- "High ultra-processed food patterns are a warning signal."
- "Replace routine sugary drinks, processed meats, and low-fiber packaged snacks with fiber-rich staples where possible."
- "Processing is not all-or-nothing; focus on the pattern."
- "Food systems shape choices."
- "Evidence is suggestive and growing, but not every packaged food is the same."

Avoid:

- "All processed food causes cancer."
- "Avoid every additive."
- "If you eat clean, you are safe."
- "People get cancer because they buy the wrong food."
- "UPF evidence replaces screening, vaccination, tobacco cessation, or medical care."

## Infographic Implications

The public visual should show:

- A "pattern warning" card, not a purity-test card.
- A cancer-evidence card with cautious language: linked, suggestive, not final.
- A replacement card: more minimally processed, fiber-rich staples.
- A systems box for price, shelf life, marketing, work, school meals, disability, kitchens, and stores.
- A safety boundary: nutrition advice is not a diagnosis, screening plan, or treatment plan.

## Next Research Tasks

- Separate UPF subgroups: sugary drinks, processed meats, ready meals, packaged sweets, refined grain products, yogurts/dairy desserts, and fiber-rich packaged foods.
- Add additive-specific pages only where human evidence is strong enough, starting with nitrites/nitrates and selected emulsifiers.
- Add affordable swap examples by region without shaming cultural foods or poverty constraints.
- Compare countries that include NOVA/UPF language in dietary guidelines with countries that use nutrient-only guidance.
