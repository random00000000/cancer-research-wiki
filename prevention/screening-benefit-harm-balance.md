## Plain-Language Summary

Cancer screening means looking for cancer, or precancerous changes, in people who do not have symptoms. Some screening programs reduce cancer deaths, and some can prevent cancer by finding and removing precancerous lesions. But screening also has harms: false alarms, missed cancers, overdiagnosis, overtreatment, radiation or procedure risk, anxiety, cost, and unequal access to follow-up.

Good public communication should not say "screening is always good" or "screening is useless." It should say: use evidence-backed screening at the right age and risk level, compare absolute benefits and harms, and treat symptoms as a medical-evaluation question rather than a screening question.

## What Is Strong Enough For Public Education

- Screening is for people without symptoms; symptoms need evaluation.
- Different cancers have different evidence. Cervical and colorectal screening can prevent some cancers by detecting precancerous changes. Lung LDCT screening can reduce lung cancer deaths in high-risk groups. Breast screening can reduce breast cancer mortality for some age/risk groups but has overdiagnosis and false-positive harms.
- More screening is not automatically better. Shorter intervals, broader eligibility, and whole-body or multi-cancer tests can increase harms if evidence and follow-up systems are weak.
- Benefits and harms should be shown with absolute numbers when possible, not only relative percentages.
- Access and follow-up matter: a screening test is not a program unless abnormal results can be evaluated and treated.

## Agent Answer Pattern

When asked about screening, answer in this order:

1. Separate screening from symptoms. Screening is for people without symptoms.
2. Name the cancer and test type; do not talk about "cancer screening" as one thing.
3. Give the benefit side: death reduction, earlier detection, or precancer removal when evidence supports it.
4. Give the harm side: false positives, false negatives, overdiagnosis, overtreatment, procedure/radiation risk, cost, anxiety, and inequitable follow-up.
5. Say what changes the answer: age, anatomy, family history, genetics, prior results, exposures, immune status, local guidelines, and access to follow-up.
6. Encourage clinician or public-health guidance for personal timing and eligibility.

## Public Wording

Use:

- "Screening is for people without symptoms; symptoms need medical evaluation."
- "Ask what this test prevents, what it detects early, and what harms it can cause."
- "Good screening needs follow-up access, not just a test."
- "Use absolute numbers when comparing benefit and harm."

Avoid:

- "Get every cancer test you can."
- "A negative screen means you are safe."
- "Screening catches all cancer early."
- "Overdiagnosis means screening is fake."

## Source Cards

### Presentation Of Benefits And Harms In Cancer Screening Guidelines

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40147829/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12527616/
- Source type: systematic guideline review
- Reusable claims:
  - Cancer screening guidelines often present benefits and harms inconsistently or incompletely.
  - Balanced absolute benefit-harm communication is needed for informed decisions.
- Caution:
  - This review focuses on Korean guidelines; pair it with broader guideline-communication studies for global claims.

### Presentation Of Benefits And Harms In US Cancer Screening Guidelines

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26917630/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5009951/
- Source type: guideline communication analysis
- Reusable claims:
  - Many US screening guidelines did not present absolute effects for both benefits and harms.
- Caution:
  - Guideline practices may have changed since publication; current recommendations still need checking.

### Estimated Lifetime Gained With Cancer Screening Tests

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37639247/
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10463170/
- Journal: JAMA Internal Medicine
- Year: 2023
- Source type: systematic review / meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials
- Reusable claims:
  - The paper argues that common cancer screening tests do not consistently show lifetime extension in trial evidence, with possible exception for sigmoidoscopy.
  - Screening claims should distinguish disease-specific mortality, all-cause mortality, and lifespan gain.
- Caution:
  - The conclusion is debated. Use it to improve precision, not to dismiss evidence-backed screening programs.

### Quantification Of Overdiagnosis In Randomized Trials

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36963292/
- Source type: overview and re-analysis of systematic reviews
- Reusable claims:
  - Overdiagnosis is a real harm in cancer screening and trial designs often underestimate it.
  - Estimates vary across cancer types and screening programs.
- Caution:
  - Overdiagnosis estimates are methodologically difficult and should be presented as ranges, not one universal number.

### Low-Dose CT Lung Screening Evidence Review

- JAMA: https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2777242
- Source type: evidence report / systematic review
- Reusable claims:
  - LDCT screening can reduce lung cancer mortality in high-risk people.
  - Harms include false positives, additional tests, invasive procedures, overdiagnosis, and radiation exposure.
- Caution:
  - Eligibility criteria and high-quality follow-up are central to benefit.

### Colorectal Endoscopy Screening Meta-Analysis

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38549924/
- Full text: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2024.1364923/full
- Source type: systematic review / meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
- Reusable claims:
  - Sigmoidoscopy and colonoscopy screening reduce colorectal cancer incidence and mortality in randomized trial evidence.
- Caution:
  - The relative contribution of invitation, adherence, test type, and follow-up colonoscopy matters.

### Organized Cervical Screening Review

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31980322/
- Source type: systematic review
- Reusable claims:
  - Organized cervical cancer screening reduced cervical cancer mortality in European implementation studies.
- Caution:
  - Program quality, HPV vaccination, test method, and follow-up access change results.

## Infographic Implications

The flagship public infographic should keep a short line about recommended screening and vaccines. Use the focused screening benefit-harm visual when the reader needs more nuance:

- Screening is cancer-specific.
- Symptoms are not screening.
- Ask for absolute benefit and harm.
- Follow-up access is part of the intervention.
